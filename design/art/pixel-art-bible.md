# Wild Frontier: Pixel Art Bible (round 3)

## Why this exists
Players described the art as "amateur, smeared, like a brush dragged over a canvas." That is accurate. The earlier sprites were painted with canvas shapes (ellipses, polygons, gradients, per-pixel noise) and then snapped to pixels, which produces blobs, mushy edges and noisy static.

From now on every asset is **drawn pixel by pixel** as a text grid with `pixmap()` (`src/render/pixmap.ts`), using the master palette `PAL`. Nothing is generated by shape-painting or noise.

## Inspiration, not copying
We study what makes **Stardew Valley** look hand-crafted (and the frontier mood of RDR2) and apply the *principles*. We never trace, reproduce or closely imitate any specific sprite, tile, character, building or UI element from those games. Every drawing must be our own design for our own 1888 western world.

## Principles to apply (the Stardew-style craft)
1. **Grid and scale.** Tiles are 16×16. A person is 16 px wide and about 24–32 px tall, with a big readable head (about 1/3 of the height). A horse is about 48–64 px long in side view. Props snap to the 16 px grid.
2. **Every pixel intentional.** No stray single pixels ("pillow shading" noise), no orphan specks, no jagged lines. Lines are 1 px and move in clean steps (1-1-2-2-3 runs), never in random zig-zags.
3. **Outline.** Characters, animals and props get a 1 px outline that is **darker and hue-tinted**, not pure black: `ink`, or the darkest step of the material's own ramp. Large flat ground tiles have no outline.
4. **Few colours per material.** 3–5 values per material, light from the **upper-left**. Shadows go cooler and slightly violet; highlights warmer and yellower. Use the named ramps in `PAL`.
5. **Clusters, not noise.** Texture comes from a few deliberate shapes: tufts of 3–5 pixels, plank seams, stone chunks, leaf clumps. Large areas stay mostly flat and calm, and detail gathers where the eye should go.
6. **Readable silhouettes.** A sprite must read as a black silhouette. Trees are big round canopy masses with 2–3 lighter clumps on the upper-left and a darker underside. Rocks are chunky with a clear top plane.
7. **Calm ground, lively actors.** Ground tiles have low contrast (2–3 close values plus sparse accents). Characters, animals and buildings carry the contrast and saturation, so they pop.
8. **Soft drop shadows.** Objects cast a simple dark, slightly transparent ellipse or flat shadow shape at the base, drawn in pixels.
9. **Tile variation.** Each ground type has 3–4 hand-drawn variants plus edge/corner transition tiles (autotiling), so the ground never looks gridded or noisy.
10. **Charm.** Small storytelling details: a flower box, a horseshoe over a door, a cat on a porch, a chipped plank. They should be sparse and deliberate.

## Workflow
- Draw the grid in TS as `const TREE = ['....aaaa....', ...]` with a key `{ a: 'leaf2', o: 'ink', ... }`.
- Build canvases once at load and cache them. Use `recolor()` for colour variants (coats, clothes, siding) instead of redrawing.
- Preview at 4× zoom (crop and `NEAREST` resize in Python PIL) and judge it like a pixel artist: silhouette, clusters, stray pixels, value grouping.
- Check in-game at the real scale next to other assets: everything must look like **one** game.
